Method 1: make a PDF look scanned in your browser

The Make PDF look scanned web tool renders every PDF page to an image in your browser, applies rotation, blur, noise, brightness, contrast and paper tone, then saves the images as a new PDF. The file never leaves your device, and the tool is free with no signup.

Under the hood, the tool uses PDF.js, which Mozilla describes as "a general-purpose, web standards-based platform for parsing and rendering PDFs", and draws each page on an HTML canvas. Because processing happens locally, the tool keeps working offline after the first load and runs in mobile browsers too.

Steps to make a PDF look scanned with the browser tool

  1. Open the scanner. Go to the free scan tool in Chrome, Edge, Safari or Firefox. No account or installation is needed.
  2. Add your PDF. Drag the file onto the page or click to choose it. The tool also accepts JPG, PNG, WebP, SVG, GIF, BMP, DOCX, XLSX, CSV, TXT, MD and HTML files.
  3. Check the live preview. The first page appears with the default effect already applied: 1° rotation, 0.5° random variance, blur 0.3, noise 0.1, grayscale, 2× resolution.
  4. Set the tilt. Keep Rotate between 0.5° and 1.5° for a believable office scan. Leave Rotate variance at 0.5–1° so every page sits at a slightly different angle, as it would after hand feeding.
  5. Tune blur and noise. Blur 0.2–0.4 softens the razor-sharp vector edges; noise 0.1–0.25 adds the speckle of a sensor. Zoom the preview to 100% before deciding.
  6. Pick the colorspace and paper tone. Gray suits contracts and letters. Choose Color for stamps, logos or colored forms, and raise Yellowish to 0.2–0.6 for off-white paper.
  7. Choose the resolution. 2× (about 144 dpi) is the balanced default; 3× (about 216 dpi) is sharper but produces a larger file.
  8. Download. Click the download button and save the new PDF. Open it once to confirm every page was processed.

Every control maps to one physical imperfection of a real scanner. The ranges and defaults below are taken directly from the tool, so you can reproduce any recipe in this guide exactly.

The 72 dpi base is not arbitrary. PDF and PostScript page geometry follows the 72-points-per-inch typographic convention, so the multiplier tells you the effective scanning resolution of each page image.

Method 3: can the print dialog make a PDF look scanned?

The print dialog built into Windows and macOS can make a PDF look scanned only partly. Microsoft Print to PDF and macOS Preview can convert pages to grayscale or compress them, but neither adds tilt, blur or noise, so the result reads as a gray digital file, not a scan.

If you are researching how to make a PDF document look scanned with nothing but your operating system, this is the route to know.

Still, the print route is most useful in two situations: when you cannot use any web tool at all, and as a preparation step. Printing a Word, Excel or web page to PDF first preserves its layout, and that PDF can then go through a full scan effect.

Windows: Microsoft Print to PDF

Windows includes a virtual printer called "Microsoft Print to PDF" that appears in the Printer list of any app. Open your PDF, press Ctrl+P, choose Microsoft Print to PDF, set Color to Black and white or Grayscale if your viewer offers it, and save the new file.

In some PDF viewers there is also a "Print as image" option under Advanced print settings. Turning it on rasterizes each page, which removes selectable text, one of the tell-tale signs of a born-digital file. The pages still sit perfectly straight and perfectly clean, though.

macOS: Preview Export with a Quartz filter

On a Mac, any app that can print can save a PDF via File → Print → PDF → Save as PDF. For a scan-like tone, open the PDF in Preview, choose File → Export, and pick a Quartz Filter such as Black & White, Gray Tone or Sepia Tone.

Apple documents that Preview's Quartz filters change a PDF's colors, for example turning a color document black and white. The Reduce File Size filter also recompresses images, which adds mild JPEG softness to embedded pictures but not to text.

Why does the print dialog give only a partial scan look?

A real scanned page is a photograph of paper: it is slightly rotated, has soft edges, a gray or cream background and random speckle. The print dialog changes color and sometimes rasterizes, but it never moves or degrades the page. Compare the outcomes side by side:

Method 4: make a PDF look like it was scanned by hand

A raster image editor lets you make a PDF look like it was scanned by hand-applying each effect: export pages as images, rotate them slightly, add noise and blur, adjust levels, then reassemble a PDF. The method gives full control over every page but takes 10–30 minutes per document.

If you want to understand how to make a PDF look like it was scanned step by step, this is the most instructive route, because you apply every effect yourself.

Any raster editor with rotation, noise, blur and levels tools will do, free or paid. The workflow is the same in all of them; only the menu names differ. Plan on doing each page separately, or record the steps as an action or macro if your editor supports one.

  1. Export pages as images. Use your PDF viewer's Export or Save as image option. Choose PNG or high-quality JPEG at 150–300 dpi; 200 dpi is a good middle ground for text.
  2. Convert to grayscale. Unless the document has colored stamps or signatures, switch the image mode to grayscale first; every later step behaves more like a real scanner.
  3. Rotate by 0.5–2°. Use arbitrary rotation, not the 90° presets. Use a different angle on each page, for example 0.7°, −0.4° and 1.1°, and fill the exposed corners with white or light gray.
  4. Add noise. Apply 2–5% monochromatic Gaussian noise. Anything above about 8% starts to look like film grain rather than a scanner sensor.
  5. Add slight blur. A Gaussian blur with a 0.3–0.6 pixel radius at 200 dpi removes the razor-sharp edges of vector text.
  6. Adjust levels. Lift the black point a little so blacks become dark gray, and pull the white point down so the paper turns faintly gray or cream.
  7. Save as JPEG. Quality 75–85% adds the subtle compression softness of a real scan without visible blocks.
  8. Reassemble the PDF. Put the images back in order and combine them into one PDF, for example in macOS Preview (select pages → Print → Save as PDF) or in Windows Photos (Print → Microsoft Print to PDF).

The effort pays off when you need something the sliders do not offer, such as a darker left margin, a shadow along a book spine or a hand-drawn crease. For a normal office look, the result is essentially the same as Method 1 with far more clicks.

Method 5: make a PDF look like a scan with your phone

A phone scan of a printout produces the most natural phone-scan look: print the PDF, then capture it with the document scanner built into iPhone Notes or Files, or into many Android camera and file apps. The phone crops, flattens and saves the page as a PDF.

On iPhone and iPad, Apple explains that you can scan paper documents with the Notes or Files app and drag the corners to fit the page before keeping the scan. Many Android phones offer a similar document mode in their built-in camera or files app; menu names vary by manufacturer.

  1. Print the PDF. Plain 80 g/m² office paper and normal print quality are enough.
  2. Use soft, even light. Daylight from a window or a ceiling light behind you avoids the hard shadow of your own hand and phone.
  3. Lay the page on a contrasting surface. A dark desk helps the scanner detect the corners automatically.
  4. Scan each page. Keep the phone parallel to the sheet; accept the auto-crop or drag the corners yourself.
  5. Choose the filter. Grayscale or "document" filters look like an office scan; the color or photo filter keeps paper texture and lighting variation.
  6. Save or share as PDF. All pages end up in a single multi-page PDF.

Method 6: print and scan the PDF for real

Printing the PDF and passing it through a flatbed or sheet-fed scanner creates a real scanned document, not an imitation. Scan at 200–300 dpi in grayscale for text, save as PDF, and expect genuine skew, dust, toner texture and slight contrast loss.

For reference, U.S. federal rules set a floor for serious digitization. NARA's regulation says of modern textual records: "For these records, produce image files at a minimum of 300 ppi sized to the source document." The same rule tolerates about 294 ppi, or 300 ppi minus 2%.

The Federal Agencies Digital Guidelines Initiative (FADGI) grades unbound documents on a star scale from about 200 ppi at one star to 400 ppi at four stars. Everyday office scans usually sit at the low end of that range, which is why 200 dpi already looks authentic for letters and forms.

Resolution
200 dpi for a typical office look; 300 dpi when a court or agency specifies it.
Color mode
Grayscale for most text; black-and-white (1-bit) for a stark fax or copier look; color only if the original has color.
Paper
Standard office paper; slightly off-white or recycled paper adds a natural tone.
Placement
Do not square the sheet perfectly against the platen guide; a tiny natural tilt is the point.

Tilt happens on its own. FADGI notes that "Skew behaviors in the captured image can often be traced to lack of parallelism between the focal plane of the camera and the platen." The Rotate variance slider in Method 1 imitates exactly this small, page-to-page skew.

Office scan: the safe default

The office recipe is the one to use when a form simply says "upload a scanned copy." Low noise and a half-degree tilt read as a well-maintained office scanner; the light contrast boost keeps small print legible. At 2× the page image is about 144 dpi, enough for comfortable on-screen reading.

Photocopy and fax-like looks

Photocopies look harsher: higher contrast crushes gray tones into black and white, and the border line imitates the dark edge copiers often leave. The fax-like recipe goes further with more noise and the 1.5× setting, about 108 dpi, close to the roughly 100 lines per inch vertical resolution of a standard-mode Group 3 fax.

Aged paper and phone-scan looks

For aged paper, switch to Color and raise Yellowish to about 0.6 while dropping brightness slightly; the page takes on a warm cream tone. The phone-scan recipe uses a larger 2° tilt and a hint of warmth, like a page photographed under indoor light. For more presets, see our scan effect guide.

How do you check that the PDF looks scanned?

To check that a PDF looks scanned, open it at 100% zoom and inspect five things: text cannot be selected, pages sit at slightly different angles, edges are soft rather than razor-sharp, the background is not pure white, and the file properties no longer name a word processor.

Real scans contain defects that archivists try to remove. NARA's regulation defines one of them plainly: "Noise is one or more undesirable image artifact(s) in a digitized record that is not part of the source material." A convincing scan look needs a small, controlled amount of that noise, not zero and not a lot.

  1. Try to select text. Press Ctrl+A or Cmd+A in your PDF viewer. In a scanned-looking PDF nothing highlights, because each page is an image.
  2. Search for a word. Ctrl+F or Cmd+F should find nothing unless OCR has been added on purpose.
  3. Compare page angles. Scroll through several pages; with rotate variance on, each should tilt a little differently.
  4. Zoom to 200%. Letter edges should look slightly soft with faint speckle, not like crisp vector outlines.
  5. Look at the margins. The paper should be very light gray or cream rather than pure white.
  6. Open Document Properties. Check the Producer and Creator fields; the tool lets you edit title, author, subject, keywords, producer and creator.
  7. Check file size. A scanned-looking PDF is larger than the original, often several times larger, because it stores images instead of text.
  8. Print one page. If the printout is readable at arm's length, the settings are fine for any upload portal.

File size matters for uploads. As a rough estimate, a text page at 2× in grayscale typically lands in the low hundreds of kilobytes, while 3× in color can be several times that. If a portal has a limit, lower the resolution to 1.5× or switch to Gray. Our guide to converting a PDF to a scanned PDF covers DPI and size trade-offs in detail.

Why does a fake scanned PDF still look fake?

A fake scanned PDF usually looks fake because one effect is overdone or one sign of a digital file survives: every page tilted identically, heavy blur, coarse noise, a pure-white background or selectable text. Fixing these five mistakes solves most of the troubleshooting cases we see.

Two more details give away a born-digital file. The first is a color logo on an otherwise gray page, which happens when only part of a document was converted; run the whole file through the same settings. The second is metadata: a Creator field naming a word processor. Edit it in the tool before downloading.

Do not overcorrect in the other direction either. Rotation, as FADGI puts it, "is introduced at capture by improper content alignment at capture", and a person feeding a scanner rarely misaligns a sheet by more than a degree or two. Big angles look staged.

Does the tool add creases, stains or shadows?

No. The tool imitates tilt, blur, noise, tone, contrast and an edge line; it does not add creases, folds, coffee stains, edge shadows or perspective warp, and it does not run OCR. If you need those physical traits, print the page and use Method 5 or Method 6, or paint them in a raster editor (Method 4).

When should you not make a PDF look scanned?

You should not make a PDF look scanned when the recipient needs searchable text, when rules ask for a text-based PDF, or when the scan look would misrepresent a document's origin. Courts, accessibility standards and many agencies prefer born-digital PDFs over images of text.

Federal courts are explicit. The Northern District of California advises: "Only documents that cannot be directly converted to PDF should be scanned." The District of Massachusetts adds that "Scanned PDF documents are generally not searchable and have a larger file size."

Accessibility is the other reason. The W3C's WCAG technique PDF7 states that "A document that consists of scanned images of text is inherently inaccessible because the content of the document is images, not searchable text." Screen reader users cannot read a scan-effect PDF unless OCR is added afterward.

Use the scan look honestlyA scan effect changes how a file looks, not whether it is authentic. Do not use it to forge or alter official documents, IDs, certificates or signatures that are not yours. When a court, employer or agency asks for a specific format, follow its rules; some require text-searchable PDFs and will reject images of text.

Good reasons to make a PDF look scanned include matching the style of a paper-based archive, preparing a design mockup or a training example, testing document-processing software on realistic input, or meeting a form that insists on a "scan" when you only have your own digital original.

We built the tool because people kept printing a PDF only to scan it straight back in. The settings that look real are small ones: a tilt of a degree or less, noise around 0.1 and a light blur. Most fake-looking results come from turning every slider up at once. And we process files locally on purpose: the documents people scan are usually private.
